Impacts of Centralized Budget Policy on National Parks: An analysis of the Federal Land Recreation Enhancement Act
Abstract Selected figures

The Federal Land Recreation Enhancement Act (FLREA) of 2004 gave park managers greater control over how user fees were collected and used within parks. At the same time, the public became concerned that it might decrease central appropriation dollars to those same parks. I digitize official National Park Service (NPS) documents to create a dataset containing over 10,000 park-year observations spanning 1986-2019, and empirically test for crowding out of central appropriations by comparing fee-charging parks to non-fee-charging parks before and after FLREA. My findings offer cautiously optimistic news to NPS policymakers: there is little evidence that FLREA led to systematic crowding out of federal appropriations. That is, there is no obvious empirical evidence that the amount of money apppropriated to parks collecting fees changed in a more or less drastic way than those parks not collecting user fees.

Stability in Centralized College Major Assignment (with Umut Dur)
Abstract Selected figures

Since 2012, North Carolina State University's College of Engineering has assigned students to majors through the Change of Degree Application (CODA), a capacity-constrained procedure that resembles the Boston mechanism with common GPA-based priorities. The Boston mechanism is well known to reward strategic misreporting, yet in a survey of NC State engineering students, 98.9% report having submitted their true first choice. Moreover, roughly nine in ten first-choice applicants in the public CODA data are admitted to that major. This paper explores why CODA seems to buck classic predictions in matching theory. We show that (i) high first-choice assignment rates are uninformative about truthfulness, because under complete information every equilibrium of the Boston mechanism assigns each student her submitted first choice and reproduces the deferred acceptance outcome; (ii) under uncertainty, a student has an incentive to shade her list only if her second choice is also oversubscribed in the first round, and that incentive shrinks as the value of her fallback rises; and (iii) certain institutional features of CODA—congestion confined to a few majors, stable and publicized admission statistics, a guaranteed engineering seat for first-year engineering students, and repeated application cycles—place most students outside the region where manipulation can improve payoffs. Descriptive evidence from seven years of CODA statistics is consistent with this account.

Recreation Welfare Effects of Climate-Driven Species Redistribution
Abstract Selected figures

Ocean warming is redistributing marine fish populations poleward, but the anglers who pursue them are tied to fixed shoreline access points. This paper estimates the welfare consequences of that spatial mismatch for shore-based recreational anglers along the U.S. Atlantic and Gulf coasts. Using a nested logit model of site choice covering 2,632 public access points from 2004--2024, I link local fish biomass observed in bottom trawl surveys. Doing this endogenizes the role that fish stocks have on anglers' recreation decisions. Combining the estimated response with species distribution projections under RCP 2.6 and RCP 8.5 across sixteen general circulation models, I simulate angler welfare through 2100. The results identify which coastal regions bear the cost of climate-driven species redistribution and quantify a channel of climate damage that weather-focused recreation studies have not measured.
